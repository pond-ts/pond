# Volume history

Thirty-six years of one network's traffic, on a **logarithmic** axis — because
the record runs from **19 GB a month to 262 PB a month** and there is no linear
axis on which both ends are visible. The question is not "how much this month"
but "is the shape still the shape", and the answer is a straight line.

</ChartExample>

Four things to try, in this order:

1. **Switch Scale to Linear.** Two decades of history flatten onto the floor.
   Switch back; that is the entire argument for `<YAxis scale="log">`.
2. **Turn Trend on to Exponential** with Time on `All`. The fit is a **straight
   line**, because a straight line on a log axis _is_ exponential growth — and
   the caption under the chart prints what it found: **+51.9% a year, doubling
   every 20 months**, held for three decades. Switch to `Linear` and the same
   least-squares idea bends badly, which is the visual proof that the growth is
   multiplicative rather than additive.
3. **Step the Month back and forward.** The shaded band is the month the summary
   below is about, drawn over that month's real calendar span.
4. **Hover a line.** Each series gets a dot and its value in bytes at that
   month — `63.9 PB`, not `6.3 e16` and not a logarithm.
5. **Drag to pan, wheel to zoom.** The window is clamped to the record plus the
   two years of empty margin the trend extrapolates into.

Times are **UTC**, which is how the record is published; the axis renders in
your local zone, so at the tightest zoom a band can sit a few hours off the
month it names.

## The data

**This one is real.** Most of the Gallery runs on modelled fixtures that say so;
this is measured traffic from [ESnet](https://www.es.net/), the US Department of
Energy's Energy Sciences Network — the science network connecting the national
laboratories to each other and to CERN. It is the same series ESnet's own
"Volume History" page draws, supplied by ESnet and committed as a fixture.

**439 months, 1990-01 to 2026-07, on a complete monthly grid** — no holes.
Three inbound series in bytes:

| Column   | What it is                                    | Starts      | Months |
| -------- | --------------------------------------------- | ----------- | ------ |
| `total`  | Every byte carried inbound that month         | **1990-01** | 439    |
| `oscars` | OSCARS — bandwidth-reserved circuits          | **2009-01** | 211    |
| `lhcone` | LHCONE — the LHC experiments' overlay network | **2015-01** | 139    |

Three quirks matter, and all three survived into the fixture on purpose.

**The starts are staggered, and the gap is real.** LHCONE traffic did not exist
before 2015, so the months before it are **absent** — `required: false` plus
`null`, which is a different claim from zero. It is also the only claim a log
axis can draw: zero has no position on one. The lines simply begin partway
across the plot.

**The span is 7.1 decades.** 19 GB in January 1990; 262 PB at the peak in
November 2025. That is a factor of **10.4 million**, and it is the reason this
page exists.

**The last year is down.** Total inbound fell **18.3%** year-on-year to July
2026 and OSCARS fell **59.2%**, while LHCONE was up **6.15%**. A chart that only
ever showed the long climb would be flattering it; the summary table is where
that shows.

The fixture is derived by
[`website/scripts/fixtures/esnet-volume.mjs`](https://github.com/pond-ts/pond/blob/main/website/scripts/fixtures/esnet-volume.mjs)
from the raw export, which stays in `packages/charts/test-data/` and never
reaches the browser: the three `_out` columns are dropped (the chart draws
inbound), values are rounded to six significant figures, and the `YYYY-MM`
index becomes a start month plus a count.

## Build it

The minimal version is a line chart with one word changed:

```tsx
<ChartContainer range={range} width={width} theme={theme}>
  <ChartRow height={300}>
    <YAxis id="bytes" scale="log" format={formatBytes} />
    <Layers>
      <LineChart series={series} column="total" as="primary" />
      <LineChart series={series} column="lhcone" as="secondary" />
      <LineChart series={series} column="oscars" as="context" />
    </Layers>
  </ChartRow>
</ChartContainer>
```

`scale="log"` is the word. Everything else about the chart is unchanged, and —
this is the part worth knowing — **`format` still formats the value**. The
transform lives in the scale, not in the data, so a tick says `1 PB` and
hovering December 2016 puts `63.9 PB` on the total's line, never a logarithm.
The obvious workaround, plotting a `log10` column on a linear axis, makes every
readout, `Baseline` and axis indicator lie.

The finished chart uses `cursor="flag"` rather than `"line"`, and the
difference is not cosmetic: `"line"` is a bare vertical rule with **no value
chips at all**. Reading three series at one month is the point of hovering
here, so `"flag"` — a dot and a value on each line — is the mode that does it.

Two notes on `format` on a log axis specifically:

- **Pass a function, not a d3 specifier.** `'.3s'` would print `197.8P`, which
  is not a byte count anyone writes. It is also actively broken here: d3's log
  scale returns the **empty string** from `tickFormat` for any value that is not
  one of its own significant ticks, so a cursor readout on a real sample comes
  out blank. A function is applied verbatim to every value.
- **Hoist it.** An inline `format={(v) => …}` is a fresh reference every render
  and re-registers the axis each frame.

### The month band

Each point is a whole month's total, so the highlight band is the span that
point stands for — and pond has the calendar to say so, rather than a nominal
30 days:

```tsx
const marked = TimeRange.fromCalendar('month', '2026-07', { timeZone: 'UTC' });

<Region
  from={marked.start}
  to={marked.endMs}
  label="Jul 2026"
  selectable={false}
/>;
```

`selectable={false}` makes it inert background context — it is a readout of the
stepper, not something to click.

### The trend

Ordinary least squares over the **visible** months, on the values for Linear and
on their logarithms for Exponential:

```tsx
// exponential: fit ln(y) = a + b·x, then draw exp(a + b·x)
const y = kind === 'exponential' ? Math.log(value) : value;
```

The fit becomes its own one-column `TimeSeries`, drawn as a fourth line with
`as="trend"` — a role whose entire job is `dash: [5, 4]`, in the same hue as the
series it models. `LineStyle.dash` is the register for a **modelled** line, and
that distinction is doing real work here: an extrapolation past the last sample
must not read as a measurement.

Two things the fit forces you to decide, and the example decides both in the
source:

- **A linear fit runs negative** over a long window. Over the whole record it
  is `−43.1 PB + 356 TB × months`, so it stays below zero until **February
  2000**. A negative number has **no position on a log axis**, so those months
  are emitted as `null` (the trend column is `required: false`) and the dashed
  line simply starts where the model becomes positive — which is worth seeing,
  because it is the fit telling you it is the wrong model.
- **The trend is a layer, so it joins the auto-fit.** Letting a linear fit's
  negative early values pick the bottom of a log axis would be absurd, so this
  example computes the domain itself: the data sets the floor, the trend may
  only widen the top.

### The summary

The table under the chart is `<BarList>` — the list family renders a real
`<table>`, which is what a per-series summary is. The bar is each row's share of
the month's total, on one shared `domain` pinned to that total, so `Total` is a
full track and the other three are directly comparable:

```tsx
<BarList
  rows={summaryRows(month, theme)}
  columns={[{ column: 'bytes', as: 'muted' }]}
  domain={[0, total]}
  after={SUMMARY_CELLS}
  theme={theme}
/>
```

`BarListColumn.as` is a property of the **column**, not the row, so a
one-bar-per-row list has one colour for every row. Rather than fight that, the
two channels are split: the bar carries magnitude in a neutral tone, and the
swatch beside each name carries identity — read from the same `line` role the
chart draws that series with, so the table cannot drift out of step with the
plot.

**Normal traffic is derived**, not a column: `total − lhcone − oscars`. That the
arithmetic closes is what pins the whole table to the inbound side of the
export.

## Options to try

| Option                                      | What it does                                                             | Reach for it when                                                           |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `<YAxis scale="log">`                       | Base-10 axis; ticks land on decades and `format` still formats the value | Data spanning more than about two orders of magnitude                       |
| `<YAxis ticks={[…]}>`                       | Your tick positions **and** your labels, driving gridlines too           | Any log axis today — see the note below                                     |
| `<LineChart as="trend">` + `LineStyle.dash` | A dashed line in the modelled register                                   | A fit, a forecast, a smoothed estimate — anything that is not a measurement |
| A fourth `<LineChart column="normal">`      | Draws the derived remainder as a line as well as a table row             | You want the three components to visibly sum to the total                   |
| `<ChartContainer grid={false}>`             | Drops the gridlines                                                      | Dense data where the rules compete with the lines                           |
| `<ChartContainer panZoom="pan">`            | Drag to pan without wheel-zoom                                           | A page where trapping the reader's scroll would be rude                     |

:::note Explicit ticks are not optional yet

This example passes `<YAxis ticks>` rather than letting the scale pick, and that
is a workaround, not a preference. `scaleLog.ticks(count)` is close to a **step
function** of the count rather than a target: on this data and domain,
`ticks(7)` returns 7 values and `ticks(8)` returns **64**. Since the count is
derived from the row's pixel height, a 300px row and a 400px row can differ by a
factor of nine in gridline count. Until that is fixed in the axis, compute the
decades you want and pass them — `ticks` drives both the labels and the row's
gridlines, so they cannot disagree.

Two more log-axis limits worth knowing now: `<AreaChart>` and stacked
`<BarChart>` have unguarded paths to `NaN` when a value or a stack base is zero,
so keep a log axis to **lines** for the moment.

:::
