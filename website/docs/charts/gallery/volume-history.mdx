# Volume history

Thirty-six years of one network's traffic, on a **logarithmic** axis — because
the record runs from **19 GB a month to 262 PB a month** and there is no linear
axis on which both ends are visible. The question is not "how much this month"
but "is the shape still the shape", and the answer is a straight line.

import ChartExample from '@site/src/components/ChartExample';
import GalleryVolumeHistory from '@site/src/examples/gallery-volume-history';

## The chart

<ChartExample name="gallery-volume-history" height={620} collapsed>
  <GalleryVolumeHistory width={720} height={300} />
</ChartExample>

Five things to try, in this order:

1. **Switch Scale to Linear.** Two decades of history flatten onto the floor.
   Switch back; that is the entire argument for `<YAxis scale="log">`.
2. **Turn Trend on to Exponential** with Time on `All`. The dashed line is
   **not a trend through the data** — it is a _forecast_. The model is fitted
   only on the months **left** of the marker and then projected forward from
   it, while the real series stays drawn over the top. So the two lines share a
   starting point and then come apart, and the gap between them is forecast
   error you can read straight off the chart.
3. **Read the caption, then drag the marker.** Fitted on 1990→2008, the model
   projects **73.7 EB** for July 2026 against an actual **197.82 PB** —
   **373× over**, so far over that the dashed line leaves the top of the plot.
   Drag the split right and the overshoot shrinks, but slowly: even splitting
   at 2024 it is still **13.7×**, because the fit window still begins in 1990
   and the 1990s dominate it.
4. **Now narrow the window instead** — switch Time to `5Y`. Same machinery,
   same default split, and it projects **213 PB** against 197.82 PB, **within
   8%**. That is the actual finding: ESnet's growth decelerated so hard that
   _which years you fit_ matters far more than _where you split them_, and a
   single line drawn through all 439 months hides it by averaging two eras.
5. **Step the Month, hover a line** (with Trend off — see below), **drag to
   pan, wheel to zoom.** Hovering gives each series a dot and its value in
   bytes at that month — `63.9 PB`, not `6.3 e16` and not a logarithm.

**Why this chart wants a log scale, precisely:** an exponential fit is a
straight line on a log axis, so a projection that is _k_ times too high sits a
constant `log₁₀ k` above the real line — the vertical gap **is** the ratio, at
every point, regardless of how big the numbers have got. On a linear axis the
same overshoot is an unreadable vertical explosion.

**One deliberate trade, so it isn't a surprise:** while a trend is on, the split
marker is in edit mode, and pond suppresses a row's data cursor whenever any
mark is being edited. So hover-to-read and drag-the-split are alternatives, not
both at once. Pan and zoom keep working either way. That is three gestures live
at a time, which is the most this chart can carry honestly — the
[gesture budget](#the-gestures) below says what was dropped and why.

Times are **UTC**, which is how the record is published; the axis renders in
your local zone, so at the tightest zoom a band can sit a few hours off the
month it names.

## The data

**This one is real.** Most of the Gallery runs on modelled fixtures that say so;
this is measured traffic from [ESnet](https://www.es.net/), the US Department of
Energy's Energy Sciences Network — the science network connecting the national
laboratories to each other and to CERN. It is the same series ESnet's own
"Volume History" page draws, supplied by ESnet and committed as a fixture.

**439 months, 1990-01 to 2026-07, on a complete monthly grid** — no holes.
Three inbound series in bytes:

| Column   | What it is                                    | Starts      | Months |
| -------- | --------------------------------------------- | ----------- | ------ |
| `total`  | Every byte carried inbound that month         | **1990-01** | 439    |
| `oscars` | OSCARS — bandwidth-reserved circuits          | **2009-01** | 211    |
| `lhcone` | LHCONE — the LHC experiments' overlay network | **2015-01** | 139    |

Three quirks matter, and all three survived into the fixture on purpose.

**The starts are staggered, and the gap is real.** LHCONE traffic did not exist
before 2015, so the months before it are **absent** — `required: false` plus
`null`, which is a different claim from zero. It is also the only claim a log
axis can draw: zero has no position on one. The lines simply begin partway
across the plot.

**The span is 7.1 decades.** 19 GB in January 1990; 262 PB at the peak in
November 2025. That is a factor of **10.4 million**, and it is the reason this
page exists.

**The last year is down.** Total inbound fell **18.3%** year-on-year to July
2026 and OSCARS fell **59.2%**, while LHCONE was up **6.15%**. A chart that only
ever showed the long climb would be flattering it; the summary table is where
that shows.

The fixture is derived by
[`website/scripts/fixtures/esnet-volume.mjs`](https://github.com/pond-ts/pond/blob/main/website/scripts/fixtures/esnet-volume.mjs)
from the raw export, which stays in `packages/charts/test-data/` and never
reaches the browser: the three `_out` columns are dropped (the chart draws
inbound), values are rounded to six significant figures, and the `YYYY-MM`
index becomes a start month plus a count.

## Build it

The minimal version is a line chart with one word changed:

```tsx
<ChartContainer range={range} width={width} theme={theme}>
  <ChartRow height={300}>
    <YAxis id="bytes" scale="log" format={formatBytes} />
    <Layers>
      <LineChart series={series} column="total" as="primary" />
      <LineChart series={series} column="lhcone" as="secondary" />
      <LineChart series={series} column="oscars" as="context" />
    </Layers>
  </ChartRow>
</ChartContainer>
```

`scale="log"` is the word. Everything else about the chart is unchanged, and —
this is the part worth knowing — **`format` still formats the value**. The
transform lives in the scale, not in the data, so a tick says `1 PB` and
hovering December 2016 puts `63.9 PB` on the total's line, never a logarithm.
The obvious workaround, plotting a `log10` column on a linear axis, makes every
readout, `Baseline` and axis indicator lie.

The finished chart uses `cursor="flag"` rather than `"line"`, and the
difference is not cosmetic: `"line"` is a bare vertical rule with **no value
chips at all**. Reading three series at one month is the point of hovering
here, so `"flag"` — a dot and a value on each line — is the mode that does it.

Two notes on `format`:

- **A function reads better than a specifier here.** `'.3s'` prints `197.8P`,
  which is not a byte count anyone writes; a function gives `197.82 PB`. (A
  specifier also used to come out **blank** on a log axis for any value that
  wasn't one of d3's own significant ticks — that is fixed, but it is the
  reason this example's formatter is a function and worth knowing if you are
  pinned to an older version.)
- **Hoist it.** An inline `format={(v) => …}` is a fresh reference every render
  and re-registers the axis each frame.

Note also what the axis is _not_ given: no `ticks`. The log axis picks whole
decades itself and thins them to the row's height, so 300px of row gets
`10 GB · 1 TB · 100 TB · 10 PB · 1 EB` and a six-month window gets
`10 PB · 100 PB · 1 EB`.

### The month band

Each point is a whole month's total, so the highlight band is the span that
point stands for — and pond has the calendar to say so, rather than a nominal
30 days:

```tsx
const marked = TimeRange.fromCalendar('month', '2026-07', { timeZone: 'UTC' });

<Region
  from={marked.start}
  to={marked.endMs}
  label="Jul 2026"
  selectable={false}
/>;
```

`selectable={false}` makes it inert background context — it is a readout of the
stepper, not something to click.

### The backtest, and the marker that splits it

Ordinary least squares on the values for Linear, on their logarithms for
Exponential:

```tsx
// exponential: fit ln(y) = a + b·x, then draw exp(a + b·x)
const y = kind === 'exponential' ? Math.log(value) : value;
```

**The marker is the split point between evidence and forecast.** Two windows,
and they do not overlap:

```tsx
const fit = fitTrend(kind, first, origin); //  fitted on [first visible, marker]
const line = trendLine(fit, origin, rightEdge); // drawn on [marker, right edge]
```

That non-overlap is the whole design. A model fitted on the months it is then
drawn over cannot be wrong about them, so the line would hug the data by
construction and mean nothing. Fitted on the data _before_ the marker, it is
genuinely out of sample, the real series stays drawn on top of it, and the
divergence is measurable — which is what the caption under the chart reports.

The line starts **at** the marker rather than at the start of the fit window,
so everything dashed is a forecast and nothing dashed is a retrodiction. No
legend needed to say which half is which.

```tsx
<Marker
  at={volumeMonthStart(origin)}
  label={`Forecast from ${monthLabel(origin)}`}
  editing
  indicator
  onChange={(at) => setTrendFrom(snapToMonth(monthGrid, at))}
/>
```

`editing` is what makes a mark draggable, `onChange` reports the new `at`
(controlled — wire it back), and `indicator` pins the same instant to the x
axis as a pill.

**Snap the value yourself.** A dragged mark snaps via `snapToGuides`, which
follows _other marks'_ x-positions and session boundaries — **not** any cursor
bucketing — so it will happily land on 14 March. The months come from pond's
calendar, realized over the visible window:

```tsx
const MONTH_SEQUENCE = Sequence.calendar('month', { timeZone: 'UTC' });
const monthGrid = MONTH_SEQUENCE.bounded(new TimeRange({ start, end }));
```

`Sequence.calendar('month')`, not a duration — months are 28–31 days, so there
is no month duration to step by. `DurationUnit` stops at `d`, and `'m'` there
means **minutes**.

**Changing the time range re-seats the split** on the new window's **midpoint**,
so every preset gets a fit window and a test window of comparable size. The
alternative considered was the first month all three series carry data
(2015-01), which is a real date with a real meaning — but it is a _fixed_ date,
so on `6M`/`1Y`/`5Y` it falls outside the window entirely and clamps to the
edge, leaving nothing to fit. The midpoint is also neutral: it asserts no claim
that anything changed on that date. The reader supplies that claim by dragging.

**Fewer than three months before the split and there is no fit at all** — two
points define a line exactly, which is a ruler, not a forecast. The chart draws
no projection then and says so where the caption goes (_"A fit needs at least
three months of data before the marker…"_), because a line that silently
vanishes reads as a bug and the fix — drag right — is not guessable. The marker
itself stays put in that state, since dragging it is the only way out.

The projection becomes its own one-column `TimeSeries`, drawn as a fourth line
with `as="trend"` — a role whose entire job is `dash: [5, 4]`, in the same hue
as the series it models. `LineStyle.dash` is the register for a **modelled**
line, and that distinction is load-bearing here: half this chart is a
measurement and half is a guess, and they are the same colour.

Two things the projection forces you to decide, and the example decides both in
the source:

- **A projection can leave the domain, in both directions.** The trend column
  is `required: false` so a non-positive projected value becomes a gap rather
  than a `NaN` coordinate — a declining linear fit crosses zero if you extend
  it far enough, and zero has no position on a log axis. Being straight about
  it: with the fit anchored at the window's first month, ESnet's data rises on
  every reachable window, so this guard is **defensive here, not exercised** —
  you will not see it fire on this page. It is one line and it is right; it is
  just not a demonstration.
- **The projection is a layer, so it joins the domain auto-fit** — and it must
  not win. A backtest can miss by orders of magnitude: fitted 1990→2008 the
  exponential projects **73.7 EB**, two and a half decades above anything ever
  measured. Letting that set the ceiling would squash 36 years of real data
  into the bottom fifth of the plot to make room for a line that is _wrong_.
  So the example computes the domain itself: **data sets the floor, and the
  projection may lift the ceiling by at most one decade** (half again on a
  linear axis). Past that the dashed line leaves the top of the plot, which is
  the honest rendering of a forecast that missed by more than the chart is
  tall. There is no per-layer opt-out of the fit — see the note below.

### The summary

The table under the chart is `<BarList>` — the list family renders a real
`<table>`, which is what a per-series summary is. The bar is each row's share of
the month's total, on one shared `domain` pinned to that total, so `Total` is a
full track and the other three are directly comparable:

```tsx
<BarList
  rows={summaryRows(month, theme)}
  columns={[{ column: 'bytes', as: 'muted' }]}
  domain={[0, total]}
  after={SUMMARY_CELLS}
  theme={theme}
/>
```

`BarListColumn.as` is a property of the **column**, not the row, so a
one-bar-per-row list has one colour for every row. Rather than fight that, the
two channels are split: the bar carries magnitude in a neutral tone, and the
swatch beside each name carries identity — read from the same `line` role the
chart draws that series with, so the table cannot drift out of step with the
plot.

**Normal traffic is derived**, not a column: `total − lhcone − oscars`. That the
arithmetic closes is what pins the whole table to the inbound side of the
export.

## The gestures

A chart this interactive runs out of gestures before it runs out of ideas. This
one wanted four — hover-to-read, pan, wheel-zoom, and drag-the-marker — plus a
bucketed region cursor to click a month with. It ships **three at a time**, and
the two that were cut were cut for measured reasons rather than taste:

| Gesture                       | Status                        | Why                                                                                                       |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Wheel-zoom                    | **kept**                      | Bounded to the record plus the trend's margin                                                             |
| Drag to pan                   | **kept**                      | No region-drag to arbitrate against, so no `regionSelectModifier` needed                                  |
| Hover reads all three series  | **kept — while Trend is off** | Any mark in edit mode forces the row's cursor to `'none'`, and the split marker is an editing mark        |
| Drag the fit/forecast split   | **kept — while Trend is on**  | The other half of the same trade                                                                          |
| Click a month (region cursor) | **dropped**                   | `cursorSequence` applies only to `cursor="region"`, which draws a band and **no value chips** — see below |

The region cursor is the interesting cut, because it _works_: give the container
`cursor="region"` and `cursorSequence={Sequence.calendar('month')}` and the band
under the pointer is the real calendar month, and a **plain click** — no drag —
fires `onRegionSelect` with exactly that month (measured here:
`[2016-12-01T00:00Z, 2017-01-01T00:00Z]`). It is a lovely way to pick a bucket.

It still lost, twice over. Adopting it costs the per-series byte readout, which
is the thing that demonstrates a log axis formatting values rather than
logarithms; and it is suppressed anyway whenever the marker is draggable, which
is precisely when you would be clicking. Two of the four cannot be on screen
together by construction, so the chart takes the readout and moves the marker by
dragging it.

## Options to try

| Option                                      | What it does                                                             | Reach for it when                                                           |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `<YAxis scale="log">`                       | Base-10 axis; ticks land on decades and `format` still formats the value | Data spanning more than about two orders of magnitude                       |
| `<LineChart as="trend">` + `LineStyle.dash` | A dashed line in the modelled register                                   | A fit, a forecast, a smoothed estimate — anything that is not a measurement |
| `<Marker editing onChange>`                 | A draggable mark whose position is a model parameter                     | The reader should be able to ask the question, not just read your answer    |
| `Sequence.calendar('month')`                | A real calendar grid to snap to                                          | Any month/week/day snapping — there is no month _duration_                  |
| A fourth `<LineChart column="normal">`      | Draws the derived remainder as a line as well as a table row             | You want the three components to visibly sum to the total                   |
| `<ChartContainer grid={false}>`             | Drops the gridlines                                                      | Dense data where the rules compete with the lines                           |
| `<ChartContainer panZoom="pan">`            | Drag to pan without wheel-zoom                                           | A page where trapping the reader's scroll would be rude                     |

:::note Keep a log axis to lines, for now

`<AreaChart>` and stacked `<BarChart>` have unguarded paths to `NaN` when a
value or a stack base is zero — a stacked bar silently loses its bottom segment,
and a filled area with a zero in it throws from `createLinearGradient`. Lines
are safe, which is all this chart needs.

Relatedly: a **zero** does not break a line the way a gap does. `.defined()`
tests the value, and `0` is finite, so neighbours are joined straight through
it. This record has no zeroes — its absences are genuine `null`s — but on a log
axis a zero has no position, so the two cases are worth keeping apart in your
own data.

:::
